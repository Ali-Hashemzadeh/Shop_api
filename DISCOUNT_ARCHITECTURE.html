Promotion

How discounts, coupons, and campaigns price an order — and why the rules are shaped the way they are.

One sentence: Catalog stores only a regular price; the Promotion module decides, live on every read, which single automatic discount wins for each variant, and one coupon may stack on top of that once — but only after an order exists, and only until the first payment attempt freezes the amount.

What this replaces

Promotional pricing used to be a second column on the variant: product_variants.compare_at_price, a "was" price that implied a discount. That column has been dropped.

A price column cannot express any of the things a real promotion needs:

Requirementcompare_at_pricePromotion module
One sale spanning many productsEdit every variant by handOne rule, many targets
Starts Friday, ends SundayNot expressiblestarts_at / ends_at
Turn a sale off instantlyRewrite every variantis_active = false
"20% off, max 5,000,000"Not expressiblemax_discount_amount
Competing overlapping salesUndefinedDeterministic winner
Order-level voucher codesNot expressibleCoupons
Historical data is not rewritten. order_items.compare_at_price still exists and still renders for orders placed before this change. New orders always write null to it. Past orders are financial records — reconstructing them from today's rules would falsify them.

The two pricing layers

There are exactly two, and they compose in a fixed order:

regular price          100,000,000     product_variants.base_price
      │
      ▼  layer 1 — ONE automatic discount (never two)
effective price         80,000,000     20% off, evaluated live
      │
      ▼  layer 2 — ONE coupon per order, on the merchandise subtotal
payable merchandise     72,000,000     10% off the 80,000,000
      │
      ▼  + shipping + tax
order total

Layer 1 — Automatic

Always on, needs no code, and applies per variant. Many rules may match one variant; exactly one is applied. Live everywhere it is displayed, frozen the instant an order is created.

Layer 2 — Coupon

Completely dormant until a customer types a code, applies to the whole order rather than to items, and is limited to one per order. Frozen when the first payment attempt is made.

Module dependencies

Promotion sits at the bottom of the graph. It imports no Catalog, Cart, Order, or Payment model, contract, or DTO — everything it needs arrives as primitives or its own DTOs. 

Cart ──→ Catalog ──→ Promotion
Order ─→ Catalog
Order ─→ Promotion
Payment → Order
Promotion → (nothing in the business domain)
Why the arrow only points one way. Catalog asks Promotion for prices. If Promotion asked Catalog anything back — "does product 15 exist?", "what products are in category 7?" — the two would be mutually dependent and neither could be reasoned about or deployed alone. Two consequences follow directly, and both look like bugs until you know the reason:
  • Discount targets are never validated against Catalog. You can target product 999999. The row is stored and is simply inert.
  • Category ancestry is resolved by Catalog, not Promotion. Catalog passes the full ancestor chain in the pricing context; Promotion just matches ids.

Best-discount-wins

A single variant can be hit by a variant rule, several product rules, several category rules (including ones aimed at an ancestor category), and several brand rules — all at once. Every candidate is costed in rials, and the one that saves the customer the most is applied. The rest do nothing.

Worked example — base price 100,000,000

RuleTargetConfiguredActual reductionOutcome
Phones Category Salecategory10%10,000,000loses
Galaxy Launch Offerproduct20%20,000,000WINS
Base Model Rebatevariant15,000,000 fixed15,000,000loses

Effective price = 80,000,000.

Automatic discounts never stack. The three rules above do not add up to 45,000,000 off. Stacking is how a storefront accidentally sells at 55% off during an overlapping campaign — the winner-takes-all rule makes the worst case knowable: the deepest single rule you configured.
Specificity does not override savings. A 30% product rule beats a 20% variant rule. It is tempting to assume the more specific rule wins, but the customer would then pay more because you wrote a broader sale — which is never the intent.

Deterministic tie-breaking

When two rules produce the exact same rial reduction the customer pays the same either way, so the price is not at stake — but the recorded winner is. An order stores a snapshot of the rule that applied, so the choice must be reproducible forever. Applied in order until one candidate remains:

  1. Largest actual rial reduction — the only rule that affects price.
  2. Most specific matched target — variant → product → category → brand.
  3. Highest explicit priority — the operator's manual override.
  4. Lowest discount id — the oldest rule, so the answer never drifts.

If one discount reaches a variant through two of its own targets (say product 15 and category 7), it counts once, credited with the more specific of the two.

Integer basis points

Percentages are stored as basis points — hundredths of a percent — in an integer column. No float ever touches a price.

PercentageStored percentage_bps
10%1000
20%2000
12.5%1250
100%10000
 
// DiscountCalculator::percentageOf()
return intdiv($amount * $bps, 10000);

Three deliberate properties:

  • Multiply before dividing. Dividing first truncates small amounts to zero.
  • Floor, always. One documented rounding direction, so a cart total and an order total can never disagree by a rial.
  • Clamped to [0, amount]. A 100% rule or an oversized fixed amount lands the price on zero; it can never go negative.

Targets and category ancestry

One rule may name any number of products, variants, categories, and brands:

Discount: "Summer Mobile Promotion" — 20%
  targets:
    product  15, 18, 27
    variant  91, 103
    category 7, 12
    brand    4

Category targets reach downwards through the tree:

Electronics          ← a rule here…
└── Phones           ← …or here…
    └── Android      ← …discounts a product filed here

The traversal runs in both directions, for two different questions, and both are Catalog's job:

DirectionQuestionUsed by
Upwards (ancestors)Which category rules can reach this product?Pricing context
Downwards (descendants)Which products does this category rule reach?has_discount, campaign products

CategoryHierarchy loads id → parent_id for the whole tree in one query and memoizes it for the request, so neither direction ever becomes a query per level per product.

has_discount

The filter now means "at least one variant currently has an applicable active automatic discount". It is compiled into a SQL constraint on Catalog's own tables, built from the target ids Promotion publishes.

Filtering a fetched page in PHP would corrupt total, last_page, and every page after the first. The constraint has to be part of the query that counts.

One subtlety worth knowing: category_id and brand_id are nullable, and in SQL NULL IN (…) is NULL, not false. Under the NOT() used by has_discount=false that would silently drop every uncategorized, brandless product — so the constraint carries explicit IS NOT NULL guards.
Price filters and sorts keep their old meaning. min_price, max_price, sort=cheapest, and sort=most_expensive still operate on base_price. Sorting by a computed promotional price is an indexing problem, not a pricing one, and is deliberately out of scope.

Coupons — model and dormancy

A coupon is a marketing code that activates a dormant pricing rule:

Coupon "SUMMER10"  ──→  Discount (trigger_type=coupon, scope=all)
scope = all does not mean a store-wide sale. This is the single most misreadable part of the schema. It means "once activated, this applies to the entire post-automatic merchandise subtotal". Without a valid code being supplied on an order, the rule affects nothing at all.

Two shapes are legal, and nothing else is:

trigger_typescopetargetsmin_subtotal
Automaticautomatictargetedat least onerejected
Couponcouponallexactly zeroallowed

Store-wide automatic discounts are not supported: an always-on rule with no target is an unbounded sale with no obvious off switch. Coupon rules may not carry product targets, because a targeted voucher is a different product with different allocation semantics.

Codes

Coupon codes are marketing identifiers customers type — SUMMER10, NOWRUZ — so they are deliberately not generated by PublicCodeGenerator. They are trimmed and uppercased on the way in and on every lookup, restricted to A–Z 0–9 - _, max 32 characters, and uniquely indexed. A code with redemptions can never be renamed or reissued against different terms.

Stacking on automatics

One automatic discount per variant, plus one coupon per order. The coupon applies to the post-automatic merchandise subtotal:

regular            100
20% automatic  →    80
10% coupon     →    72        ✅ 10% of 80

NOT: 20% + 10% = 30% off 100 → 70    ❌
Shipping and tax are never coupon-eligible. Both the min_subtotal threshold and the percentage are measured against merchandise alone, so a coupon can never discount delivery.
A coupon is never allocated across items. A 100,000 coupon on a ten-item order does not put 10,000 on each line. It is stored once, at order level. Spreading it would invent per-item prices no rule produced — and refunds would then have to un-invent them.

The pricing freeze

An order can have many payment attempts — a failed card, a retry, a switch to cash. Every one of them must charge the same amount. So the first attempt to reach pricing finalization locks the decision and stamps payment_pricing_finalized_at.

Crucially, the absence of a coupon is frozen too — otherwise a code could be bolted on after a payment page had already quoted a total.

Frozen stateLater requestResult
SUMMER10coupon_code omittedallowed — uses frozen pricing
SUMMER10summer10allowed — normalizes to the same code

no couponcoupon_code omittedallowed
no couponany code422
Order owns this, not Payment. Payment calls OrderManagerInterface::finalizeForPayment() and then charges order.total_amount. It runs no pricing logic of its own. Only the code is accepted from the client — never an amount, a total, or a percentage.
A gateway failure does not unfreeze the order. Pricing is finalized before the amount is sent to an external gateway, and a network error afterwards leaves the freeze and the coupon reservation intact, so the retry charges the identical figure. The pending-order TTL is what eventually cleans up.

Reservation lifecycle

POST /orders/{id}/coupon/check      →  nothing persisted (advisory preview)
first payment initialization       →  RESERVED   + pricing frozen
payment attempt fails              →  RESERVED   (unchanged — order is still payable)
payment retried                    →  RESERVED   (same reservation, same amount)
order paid                         →  REDEEMED
order cancelled / expired / replaced → RELEASED

Usage limits count reserved + redeemed, never released, so an abandoned checkout returns its use to the pool.

Concurrency

Reservation locks the coupon row FOR UPDATE before counting:

lock coupon row FOR UPDATE
  ↓  re-check active / dates / rules
  ↓  count reserved + redeemed
  ↓  check global limit, then per-user limit
  ↓  write the reservation

Counting and then inserting without the lock is the classic oversell race — two checkouts both read "1 use left" and both write. A unique index on coupon_redemptions.order_id is the final backstop, and also enforces one-coupon-per-order.

Release and redeem live in shared primitives, not in four places each. Release hangs off CancelOrderAction::releaseAndCancel(), already shared by customer cancel, admin cancel, TTL expiry, and pending-order replacement. Redemption hangs off EloquentOrderManager::markAsPaid(), so an online capture and an in-person cash payment behave identically. Both operations are idempotent — a repeated gateway callback cannot double-redeem, and a release can never undo a realized sale.
A failed payment does not release the coupon. The order is still pending and still payable; dropping the claim would let someone else take the last use out from under a customer who is mid-retry.

Campaigns — merchandising

A campaign is a grouping, not a pricing rule. It answers "why is this product shown here?" — never "what does it cost?". 

Summer Sale
├─ 20% Discount    → products 1, 2, 3
├─ 10% Discount    → Samsung brand
├─ 5,000,000 fixed → variant 91
└─ 15% Discount    → Accessories category

That is the point: each product in the section can carry a different discount, instead of every "sale" product being forced to share one rule.

A campaign does not force its own discount to win. If a Summer Sale product also matches a stronger unrelated 20% rule, the product page and the campaign page both show 20%. Membership and pricing are answered independently — opening a campaign page must never change what something costs.

Campaigns may link automatic discounts only. A coupon rule is store-wide and has no product identity at all, so it could never answer the membership question; linking one is rejected.

The storefront endpoints live in Catalog, not Promotion: resolving a campaign's products needs the product tables, the category tree, and Catalog's visibility rules. Promotion publishes campaign metadata plus raw target ids; Catalog does the resolution with its own SQL. Variant targets resolve to their owning product, category targets expand to descendants, and duplicates collapse.

All six are seeded and granted to admin. The customer role receives none of them — and needs none: browsing products, viewing campaigns, checking a coupon on your own order, and paying are all protected by authentication and ownership, never by a promotion permission.

Coupon and campaign mutations are split from discount CRUD deliberately, so a marketing operator can be given code and merchandising control without the ability to rewrite pricing rules.

Gotchas

Live before the order, frozen after. Product, cart, and campaign prices are recomputed on every read. Disable a discount and the next cart refresh reflects it. Once an order exists its item pricing never moves again — not even when payment is initialized.
Checkout re-reads prices. The cart is a preview. If a promotion ends between the cart page and checkout, the order is created at the new price.
Nothing promotional is stored on a cart. No coupon column, no cached discount. Coupons only exist once there is an order to attach one to.
Deleting a discount is a soft-delete. It stops pricing immediately, and every redemption, order snapshot, and payment amount is left untouched.
Zero-value orders are rejected. A coupon that would take the payable total to zero or below returns 422 rather than inventing a free-order payment flow. The rejection rolls back the reservation, so the coupon is not silently consumed.
Batch, never per item. Product listings, cart reads, and checkout each evaluate promotions in one call. A ten-product page costs the same two Promotion queries as a one-product page; a regression test asserts this.
